Artificial intelligence-empowered virtual cell models represent an emerging 
approach for in silico drug discovery1–3, yet most existing approaches lack large-scale, 
time-resolved perturbation proteomics data and interpretable frameworks for 
predicting therapeutic responses. Here we generated more than 38 million temporal 
protein-abundance measurements from systematically perturbed breast cancer cell 
lines, and developed ProteinTalks, a virtual cell model. Central to ProteinTalks is the 
synergy of this large-scale dynamic proteomic resource and the model architecture, 
enabling a new pretraining framework that learns transferable dynamical latent 
representations from temporal proteome trajectories. By modelling how proteins 
respond conditionally to different perturbations, this approach enables the model  
to function as an operational tool for diverse drug discovery tasks: predicting  
drug efficacy and synergy, discovering new drug combinations, probing proteins 
associated with drug resistance, stratifying patient responses and prioritizing drug 
candidates for patient organoids. It also shows robust transferability, extending 
beyond cell lines to patient-derived organoids and clinical biopsies, generally 
achieving higher performance than the selected benchmark implementations under 
the evaluated protocols. T ogether, ProteinTalks shows how scalable pretraining  
of transferable dynamic representations enables operational, dynamics-aware, 
proteomics-based virtual cell models to advance in silico drug discovery.
In silico drug discovery represents a transformative frontier in bio-
medical research, built on predictive cellular computational models 
that simulate molecular and cellular responses to therapeutic per -
turbations. In this context, the virtual cell concept has emerged as a 
promising approach in drug discovery, in which the central challenge 
lies in accurately modelling cellular responses and dynamics. Given 
the inherent nonlinearity of cellular processes, virtual cells require 
training on real-world dynamic data to capture the true complexity 
of cellular behaviour1–3.
Recently, foundation models trained on large-scale omics datasets 
are increasingly viewed as a critical path towards an artificial intel -
ligence (AI) virtual cell. However, the capability of these models to 
represent and predict dynamic cellular behaviour remains constrained 
by the data modalities and learning architectures. Representative 
foundation models, such as Geneformer4, scFoundation5 and UCE6, 
learn general-purpose embeddings from transcriptomic snapshots 
through self-supervised pretraining. Models trained on perturbation 
transcriptomic datasets generated by Perturb-seq, such as STATE7, 
STACK8, x-Cell9 and CellOT10, further extend this strategy to model 
cellular responses to perturbations. In parallel, existing trajectory 
modelling approaches (for example, PRESCIENT 11, trajectorynet12, 
DeepRUOT13) are developed to better account for cellular dynamics. 
T ogether, these methods have substantially advanced the represen-
tation of cellular states and transitions 14. However, these methods 
are typically optimized for static cellular embeddings, perturbation 
endpoints or trajectory interpolation within predefined latent spaces 
such as principal component analysis embeddings, rather than for 
learning transferable representations from rich temporal perturbation

T o address this gap, we established a systematic temporal perturba-

in high-throughput proteomics enabled by data-independent acquisi-

high-throughput platform, we perturbed breast cancer cell lines with 
clinically relevant drugs and their combinations, yielding more than 
38 million perturbed protein measurements alongside cell viability 
data. Leveraging this large-scale dataset, we developed ProteinTalks, 
a virtual cell model that jointly learns a latent representation together 
with perturbation-conditioned dynamics through a neural ordinary 
differential equation (ODE)22,23 framework, in which the dynamics them-
selves shape the learned representation (Fig. 1). Despite using 1,000-fold 
fewer parameters and samples than existing static transcriptomic foun-
dation models, we evaluated ProteinTalks across diverse tasks, including 
cross-cell-line and cross-drug settings, transfer learning across cancer 
types, drug synergy prediction and fine-tuning for patient-derived xeno-
graft (PDX) response prediction. Further, it stratified clinical outcomes 
in 501 patients with breast cancer and prioritized drug candidates from 
a library of 3,000 repurposed drugs for triple-negative breast cancer 
(TNBC) patient-derived organoids (PDOs). Interpretability analyses 
highlight candidate proteins with limited previous functional char-
acterization in the examined drug-response contexts that align with 
experimental and clinical validation. T ogether, this work proposes Pro-
teinTalks as an operational, dynamics-aware, proteomic-based virtual 
cell model with cellular functional capability for in silico drug screening.
Perturbed breast cancer cell proteomes
T o build a virtual cell model, we generated a large-scale perturbation 
proteome dataset using 16 TNBC cell lines and 2 non-TNBC cell lines 
(Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1a). These cells were treated with 63 
US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved small-molecule 
drugs targeting several signalling pathways (Extended Data Fig. 1a 
and Supplementary Information). These drugs were classified into 5 
primary classes and 13 subclasses (Supplementary Table 1b,c). Guided 
by a published drug-combination screen24, we further selected 914 
combination–cell line tuples across these 18 breast cancer cell lines.
Previous perturbation transcriptomics studies typically measure 
transcriptomic changes at 6 h to 24 h following treatment 25,26.  To 
account for the subsequent transcription-to-translation lag, we profiled 
protein expression at 11 time points across 3 cell lines treated with 55 
single agents and 57 drug combinations, generating a multi-time-point 
dataset (mtPTDS) of 2,528 proteomic samples with a high degree of 
reproducibility across quality control samples (Supplementary Table 2 
and Extended Data Fig. 2). Uniform manifold approximation and pro-
jection (UMAP) visualization showed a prominent separation asso-
ciated with sampling time, with three temporal clusters: early (0 h, 
2 h, 4 h, 6 h), intermediate (8 h, 10 h, 12 h, 24 h) and late (36 h, 48 h, 
60 h) (Extended Data Fig. 2e). We therefore selected 6 h, 24 h and 48 h 
as representative time points to capture early, intermediate and late 
proteomic responses.
The 18 cell lines were treated with 63 FDA-approved drugs and 59 drug 
combinations for 6 h, 24 h and 48 h in biological triplicate, generating 
16,311 perturbation proteomic DIA-MS data files (roughly 6,668 h of 
mass spectrometry instrument time) and 23,482 cytotoxicity meas-
urements (Fig. 1). DIA-NN analysis yielded more than 38 million pro-
tein measurements across 5,585 protein groups, with a high degree of 
reproducibility confirmed by quality controls (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). 
Proteomic profiles showed cell-line-specific patterns (Extended Data 
Fig. 1d) that were affected by treatment duration (Extended Data 
Fig. 1e). We refer to this resource as the ProteinTalks dataset (PTDS) 
(available at db.prottalks.com).
Perturbed proteomes capture drug MOAs
T o discern whether perturbed proteomes signify drug mechanism of 
action (MOA), we first focused on the proteins directly targeted by the 
compounds. We identified 61 protein targets (48.8% of all drug targets) 
across the 63 drugs (Extended Data Fig. 3a). For example, thymidylate 
synthase (TYMS), the canonical target of capecitabine-derived fluoropy-
rimidine metabolites, increased progressively in capecitabine-resistant 
cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b). TYMS knockdown in HCC1143 cells 
restored capecitabine sensitivity (Extended Data Fig. 3c,d), validat-
ing TYMS upregulation as a resistance mechanism and suggesting that 
perturbation proteomics can offer clues about potential molecular 
mechanisms responsible for drug efficacy and resistance.
Beyond the proteins directly targeted by the drugs, we devel -
oped PertScore to quantify protein changes, and identified 823 
perturbation-related proteins (PertScore 10 or greater, false discovery 
rate (FDR) less than 0.05) (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f and Supplemen-
tary Table 3a). Pathway enrichment of these proteins showed that 
MOA-associated pathways were significantly perturbed at 6 h, whereas 
cell death related pathways were enriched at 48 h (Fig. 2a and Supple-
mentary Table 3b). Our data indicate that these proteins and pathways 
act as sentinels of phenotypic shifts in cancer cells. Drug-class-specific 
enrichment further matched expected biology: alkylating agents pref-
erentially affected DNA-damage repair, hormone therapies altered 
metabolic reprogram pathways, microtubule inhibitors modulated 
cytoskeleton organization and cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibi-
tors perturbed cell-cycle pathways.
Next, we narrowed down to the consistently dysregulated proteins 
across perturbations within each drug type using mFuzz clustering. 
Both chemotherapies and targeted drugs increased the expression 
levels of proteins related to nucleic acid synthesis and fatty acid metab-
olism, while decreasing the expression levels of proteins involved in 
RNA processing, RNA metabolism and cell-cycle progression (P < 0.05, 
Fig. 2b). Kinase inhibitors specifically upregulated proteins linked to 
MET, NOTCH4 and mTOR signalling (P < 0.05, Fig. 2b). Our data dem-
onstrate that each compound interacted with one or more protein 
targets and induced proteome-level changes by means of both shared 
and perturbagen-specific modulation of cellular processes that reflect 
its MOA. These perturbagen-induced abundance changes varied in 
magnitude from protein to protein.
Proteome dynamics mark drug resistance
Next, we explored longitudinal changes related to drug sensitivity within 
this dataset. A total of 63 proteins showed differentially dynamic expres-
sion between CDK-inhibitor-resistant and CDK-inhibitor-sensitive 
groups (analysis of variance (ANOVA), P  < 0.05, Supplementary 
Table 3c). Specifically, 41 proteins showed an increase in expression 
following perturbation in the resistant group but decreased in the 
sensitive group (ANOVA, P < 0.05, Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3c), 
enriched in cell-cycle associated pathways, protein localization, purine 
ribonucleotide metabolic process and carbon metabolism (P < 0.05, 
Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3d). The remaining 22 proteins showed 
an inverse dynamic expression pattern (ANOVA, P < 0.05, Fig. 2c and 
Supplementary Table 3c), mainly associated with small-molecule 
transport, endomembrane system organization and the AKT signal-
ling pathway (P < 0.05, Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3d).

study comprises 18 cell lines (16 TNBC cell lines and 2 non-TNBC cell lines) treated 
with 63 FDA-approved drugs across several time points (0 h, 6 h, 24 h and 48 h). 
Each treatment condition was performed in biological triplicate, generating 
16,311 proteomic profiles and 23,482 cell viability measurements, which were 
used to pretrain ProteinTalks, a deep learning model for predicting temporal 
protein dynamics and drug responses. The architecture takes the baseline

Virtual cell modelling
On the basis of these large-scale proteomics measurements, we then 
established ProteinTalks, a virtual cell model integrating ODEs with 
perturbation-aware neural networks, pretrained on 38 million protein 
measurements from the 16,311 perturbed profiles (PTDS). The model 
contains two modules (Fig. 3a). The first module encodes the baseline 
proteome and drug targets to predict time-resolved proteome changes 
(Loss 1; Fig. 3a). The second module integrates the predicted proteome 
trajectories with drug structures to predict drug response and com-
bination synergy through multilayer perceptron (Loss 2; Fig. 3a). This 
framework supports downstream applications in drug discovery and 
enables fine-tuning in patient-derived models.
T o determine optimal temporal sampling, we trained the model 
on mtPTDS and evaluated predicted proteomic trajectories across 11 
post-treatment time points. The model recovered a gradual, ordered 
transition across the ten post-treatment states (Extended Data Fig. 4a), 
suggesting that it effectively encodes the time-dependent evolution of 
protein expression following perturbation. Increasing the number of 
time points modestly improved accuracy (P = 0.03) but decreased area 
under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) (P = 0.02) (Extended Data 
Fig. 4b–d), reflecting a trade-off between discrimination (accuracy) 
and precision–recall (AUPRC). We therefore selected four time points 
(0 h, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h) to balance these metrics (with theoretical justifi-
cation provided in the Supplementary Information), consistent with 
biological dimension reduction results (Extended Data Fig. 2e), which 
captured early-phase, intermediate-phase and late-phase proteomic 
responses to drug perturbations while maintaining sufficient dataset 
diversity and broad applications.
Prediction of drug responsiveness
When trained on the four-time-point PTDS, ProteinTalks achieved  
an accuracy of 0.86, an area under the receiver operating character-
istic (ROC) curve (AUROC) of 0.92 and an AUPRC of 0.84 on the held-out 
test set, surpassing models trained on the higher-time-resolution 
mtPTDS dataset. Model performance increased consistently as  
the training set expanded from 10% to 70% of PTDS (Extended Data 
Fig. 4e–g).
We next evaluated generalization across cellular contexts using 
leave-one-cell-line-out cross-validation. Across 17 evaluable cell lines, 
ProteinTalks achieved an average AUPRC of 0.89, an average AUROC of 
0.95 and an average accuracy of 0.90, outperforming 7 benchmark mod-
els, including 3 transcriptomics-based foundation models (GeneCom-
pass34, Geneformer4 and UCE6) and 4 conventional machine learning 
models (bootstrap aggregation, k-nearest neighbour (KNN), random 
forest and DeepSynergy35). Relative to these benchmarks, ProteinTalks 
improved AUPRC by 10–221%, AUROC by 4–98% and accuracy by 3–21% 
(Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 5).
In the leave-one-drug-out cross-validation, ProteinTalks exceeded 
transcriptomics-based foundation models by 13–23% in AUROC and 
24% in accuracy, and exceeded traditional machine learning approaches 
by 12–22% in accuracy, obtaining an average AUPRC of 0.57, an aver-
age AUROC of 0.64 and an average accuracy of 0.76 (Supplementary 
Table 6). Compared with existing models, ProteinTalks achieved higher 
prediction performance in this evaluation.
Independent tests on untrained compounds
We further evaluated whether ProteinTalks could predict responses 
to untrained compounds. T o this end, we generated an independent 
perturbation proteomics dataset (called PTNC) comprising 81 more 
anticancer compounds across 4 TNBC cell lines included in PTDS, yield-
ing 2,076 dose–response measurements to derive drug-efficacy labels. 
ProteinTalks outperformed the 3 transcriptomics-based foundation 
models, with average gains of 32% in AUPRC, 73% in AUROC and 150% in 
accuracy. Its performance (AUPRC 0.94, AUROC of 0.89, accuracy 0.85) 
clearly surpassed Geneformer (0.70, 0.51, 0.31), GeneCompass (0.72,

extend beyond its training compounds to accurately predict responses 
to untrained anticancer compounds.
Application in many cancer types
T o expand our evaluation to 4 non-breast cancer types with a substan-
tially larger set of repurposed drugs, we generated an extra independent 
perturbation proteomics dataset, PTPC, comprising 2,307 perturbation 
proteomes from 5 cancer cell lines representing melanoma, colorectal, 
lung and pancreatic cancers. These cell lines were treated with 708 
repurposed compounds, and proteomic data were collected at 6 h 
and 24 h following treatment (Supplementary Table 8), yielding 5,171 
quantified proteins with high reproducibility confirmed by several 
quality control samples (Extended Data Fig. 5).
On this dataset, ProteinTalks surpassed the three transcriptomics- 
based foundation models by 74% in AUPRC and 28% in AUROC, reach-
ing an average AUPRC of 0.41 and an average AUROC of 0.77, versus 
Geneformer (0.23, 0.56), GeneCompass (0.28, 0.72) and UCE (0.19, 
0.54) (Fig.  3d, Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Infor -
mation). These results indicate that ProteinTalks may transfer to 
non-breast-cancer cell lines for drug-response prediction.

Dynamic protein network remodelling
T o elucidate how drug perturbations reshape protein regulatory net-
works over time, we computed dynamical Shapley additive explana-
tions (SHAP) values from ProteinTalks predictions of perturbed protein 
expression at 6 h, 24 h and 48 h. We then compared SHAP attribution 
patterns between responsive and non-responsive samples within each 
drug class.
These analyses revealed drug-class-specific temporal divergence. 
Alkylating agents, HDAC inhibitors and topoisomerase inhibitors 
showed minimal divergence at 6 h but marked separation by 24 h 
(Extended Data Fig. 6a–c), reflecting delayed protein-level manifes-
tation of DNA damage and epigenetic remodelling through regula-
tory cascades. Conversely, CDK inhibitors, hormonal agents and RXR 
inhibitors showed pronounced divergence as early as 6 h, indicating 
more immediate protein-level signalling responses (Extended Data 
Fig. 6d–f). Thus, ProteinTalks-derived latent trajectories recapitulated 
biologically relevant temporal dynamics of drug response.
Prioritizing drug resistance proteins
We next used drug-efficacy SHAP values to quantify model-attributed 
protein importance for drug response by comparing effective versus 
ineffective groups. Proteins were first ranked by their average SHAP val-
ues across drugs (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 11a). 
T o further prioritize dynamically regulated proteins, we ranked pro-
teins by the temporal variation in their SHAP values across time points 
(Fig. 4a,b), highlighting proteins whose contribution to drug response 
changed most strongly over time.
Beyond known microtubule-associated proteins, aldo-keto reduc-
tase 1C3 (AKR1C3) emerged as one of the most important proteins for 
antimitotic agents (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We therefore knocked down 
AKR1C3 in BT20 cells using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and assessed 
docetaxel response using cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 4c). Knockdown 
efficiency was confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis (Extended 
Data Fig. 7b). Cytotoxicity assays revealed that AKR1C3 knockdown 
enhanced BT20 cell sensitivity to docetaxel (Δln[IC50] = 4.24) (Fig. 4d), 
supporting AKR1C3 as a ProteinTalks-prioritized protein associated 
with docetaxel response.
Further analyses showed the clinical and biological relevance 
of selected proteins among the top 100 ProteinTalks-prioritized pro-
teins (Supplementary Table 11b,c and Supplementary Information). 
These results validate that ProteinTalks effectively identifies drug 
resistance biomarkers with translational potential.
Predicting outcomes in PDX models
T o further evaluate clinical applicability, we fine-tuned ProteinTalks 
using transcriptomes from 30 patient-derived tumour cell models 
from patients with breast cancer treated with 96 compounds39 (Fig. 5a). 
Pretraining on perturbation proteomics improved performance 
compared with from-scratch training, increasing mean accuracy 
from 0.65 to 0.71, AUROC from 0.65 to 0.74 and AUPRC from 0.72 to 
0.77 (Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 12a). We also 
fine-tuned ProteinTalks using breast cancer PDX transcriptomes, 
and then evaluated the fine-tuned model in a zero-shot setting on 
140 non-breast cancer PDX transcriptomes40. ProteinTalks outper-
formed three transcriptomics-based foundation models by an average 
of 24% in AUROC (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 8b–d and Supplementary 
Table 12b,c). These results indicate the transfer of ProteinTalks to clini-
cally relevant patient-derived tumour transcriptomes.
Prognosis stratification from biopsies
T o validate patient-level clinical applicability, we evaluated our model 
on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsies to predict 
post-treatment patient prognosis. We profiled 501 treatment-naive 
FFPE breast tumour specimens from patients with TNBC who sub -
sequently received chemotherapy (TNBC-HMU), quantifying 3,583 
proteins with high reproducibility (Supplementary Table 13a,b and 
Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). ProteinTalks computed individualized risk 
scores from baseline tumour proteomes and treatment regimens 
(Fig. 5c). Median-based stratification of these scores separated patients 
into high-risk and low-risk groups with significant differences in 
recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 5d) and overall survival (Fig. 5e). For 
patients receiving multi-drug regimens beyond the model’s two-drug 
limit, we further evaluated a biomarker panel comprising the top 100 
SHAP-ranked proteins. This panel also stratified both recurrence-free 
(Extended Data Fig. 9c) and overall survival (Extended Data Fig. 9d).

Discussion
Development of virtual cell models with general applicability for in 
silico drug discovery represents a central challenge for AI-empowered 
biomedicine. Current efforts towards virtual cell models largely rely 
on large-scale transcriptomic datasets. Here we propose a comple-
mentary route grounded in large-scale, time-resolved perturbation 
proteomics and present ProteinTalks as a prototype virtual cell model. 
A key feature of ProteinTalks is the alignment between dataset collec-
tion and architecture design. Our perturbation proteomic dataset, 
comprising systematic time-course measurements across diverse 
drugs and cell lines, provides the temporally resolved supervision 
that is largely absent from existing proteomic resources. This rich 
temporal structure, in turn, enables a neural ODE-based pretraining 
framework that jointly learns both the latent representations and the 
perturbation-conditioned dynamics. In contrast to previous trajec-
tory modelling methods that perform interpolation or optimal trans-
port within predefined representations such as principal component 
analysis embeddings, ProteinTalks allows perturbation-conditioned 
trajectories to actively shape what features the model discovers. The 
resulting framework uses a comparatively compact architecture to 
model time-dependent protein-abundance changes and was evaluated 
across held-out cell lines and compounds, additional cancer cell lines 
and several patient-derived datasets.
The scale and temporal structure of PTDS are important compo -
nents of the modelling framework. This dataset represents one of the 
largest time-resolved perturbation proteomics resources reported to 
date, comprising more than 38 million protein measurements from 
approximately 16,000 perturbed proteomic samples43–45. Critically, 
unlike the static preperturbation or post-perturbation snapshots com-
mon in existing datasets, our time-series design captures the dynamic 
evolution of cellular responses. This temporal resolution, combined 
with proteomic measurement, distinguishes ProteinTalks from 
transcriptome-based foundation models4,5,7. By contrast, even very 
large-scale single-cell transcriptomic collections often remain limited 
in perturbational and contextual diversity, restricting cross-context 
generalization46. The present dataset therefore provides a foundation 
for learning latent representations that transfer across drugs, cellular 
contexts and patient-derived tumours.
The availability of extensive time-course proteomics data ena -
bled the use of a neural ODE architecture capable of learning cellular 
trajectories from temporal dynamics. Although neural ODEs have been 
previously applied in single-cell and omics analyses (for example, tra-
jectory inference and perturbation mapping), this approach remains 
largely task-specific rather than serving as a general pretraining strat-
egy for transferable dynamic embeddings 14. Many transcriptomic 
foundation models are pretrained predominantly on snapshot mea-
surements and therefore receive less direct temporal supervision than 
ProteinTalks. By contrast, our temporal measurements provide empiri-
cal constraints for modelling nonlinear changes in protein abundance 
after drug perturbation, enabling ProteinTalks to perform effectively 
across datasets with fewer parameters than transcriptomic foundation 
models. Recent benchmarks have reported that such parameter-heavy 
foundation models remain insufficient for perturbation response 
prediction15–17. Leveraging temporal proteomic supervision and a 
continuous-time modelling objective rather than parameter scale, 
ProteinTalks showed robust and broad applicability across predictive 
tasks spanning 18 breast cancer cell lines, PDX models and a cohort of 
501 patients, extending from 63 training drugs to 3,000 compounds 
and validating clinical translatability from cell lines to patient biopsies.
These model-attribution analyses also generated experimentally 
testable hypotheses about proteins associated with drug response 
and translated them into actionable biological insights, including 
experimentally validated drug-resistance-associated proteins (for 
example, AKR1C3 mediating antimitotic agent response) and 18 
chemotherapy-associated proteins with limited functional charac-
terization in cancer, such as ZFYVE19, SYNE2 and zinc finger proteins. 
By integrating large-scale temporal proteomics with dynamics-aware 
modelling, ProteinTalks prioritizes proteins and temporal attribution 
patterns for subsequent experimental investigation.
Despite these advances, this study has several limitations. Our 
DIA-MS strategy prioritized throughput over depth, yielding fewer 
proteins than ultra-deep methods43,44, although continuing AI-driven 
advances47,48 are enhancing DIA-MS data analysis. Post-translational 
modifications and multi-omics integration (transcriptomics, metabo-
lomics, epigenomics) were not included, and our dataset focused on 
cancer cell lines, particularly TNBC. Although the current framework 
captures some aspects of drug-combination structure, it does not yet 
explicitly model dose-dependent or sequence-dependent treatment 
effects, which remain important directions for future development. 
Future expansion to diverse biological contexts and harmonization 
of heterogeneous, temporally resolved multi-omics data will further 
improve mechanistic interpretability and virtual cell completeness. 
Moreover, as a module grounded in temporally resolved proteomic 
state transitions, ProteinTalks could be naturally integrated into 
broader agent-based architectures, such as those exemplified by 
CellForge49: a multi-agent framework that autonomously designs and 
refines neural network architectures for predicting single-cell perturba-
tion responses, incorporating trajectory-aware encoders and perturba-
tion diffusion modules for phenotype decoding. In such systems, the 
generalizable representations learned by ProteinTalks would serve as 
a dynamic proteomic backbone, complementing other omics-specific 
modules while autonomous agents coordinate multi-omics perception, 
biological hypothesis generation and closed-loop experimental valida-
tion. T ogether, these capabilities point towards a more comprehensive, 
interpretable and actionable AI virtual cell model capable of actively 
reasoning about and simulating cellular intervention strategies across 
biological scales.
In summary, ProteinTalks introduces a perturbation proteomics- 
based operational virtual cell model that learns sophisticated  
protein temporal dynamics from large-scale temporal data, distils 
high-dimensional perturbations into interpretable representations 
and enables broad use across translational tasks: from predicting 
drug synergies and proteins associated with drug resistance in cell 
lines to prognosis stratification and patient-specific candidate-drug 
nomination.

Methods
Cell-line panel
Nineteen cell lines (BT20, BT549, DU4475, HCC1143, HCC1395, HCC1806, 
HCC1937, HCC38, HCC70, Hs578T, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-453-1, 
MDA-MB-468, HCC1187, A375, HCT116, NCI-H1975, NCI-H2170 and 
Panc 10.05) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collec-
tion (ATCC). MDA-MB-453-2, MDA-MB-231, T47D and MCF7 were pur-
chased from Meisen. Detailed information is listed in Supplementary 
Table 1a and Supplementary Information. Two MDA-MB-453 cell lines 
from different sources are distinguished as MDA-MB-453-1 (ATCC) and 
MDA-MB-453-2 (Meisen).
Cell viability assay
Compounds were sourced from MedChemExpress and TargetMol 
(Supplementary Table 1b). IC50 values were determined using the CCK8 
cell proliferation assay (Sigma) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 
The log-phase cells (Supplementary Table 1a) were seeded in 96-well 
plates at 100 μl per well in basal medium supplemented with 10% fetal 
bovine serum. After 24 h, medium was replaced with gradient drug 
concentrations (0 μM, 0.5 μM, 5 μM, 50 μM, 200 μM) in 5% fetal bovine 
serum medium for an extra 24 h (Supplementary Table 1c). Untreated 
cells served as negative controls, with dimethylsulfoxide maintained 
below 0.5%. Cell-free wells served as blank controls. CCK8 reagent 
(10 μl, 5 mg ml−1) was then added to each well, and plates were incubated 
for 4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan 
Spectrum (BioT ek) to calculate IC50 values.
Drug-efficacy criteria were as follows: (1) cell viability should increase 
with decreasing drug concentrations (variance less than 20%). Drugs 
were deemed ineffective if viability exceeded 50% at all concentrations 
(0.5 μM, 5 μM, 50 μM, 200 μM). (2) Drugs were considered effective if 
viability at 50 μM was below 50% and further reduced to less than half 
the viability observed at 0.5 μM. Effective drugs identified by these 
criteria were subsequently tested over 72 h across ten concentrations: 
0 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, 2 μM, 4 μM, 8 μM, 16 μM, 32 μM and 50 μM. 
Synergy of drug combination was declared if either (1) the maximal 
effect gain ΔEmax ≥ 0.2 or (2) potency shift Δlog2[IC50] ≥ 3, relative to 
the Bliss reference model.
Drug treatment for proteomics preparation
Each 96-well plate included control samples (no drug treatment) and 
blank samples (medium only, no cells). Samples within each plate 
were processed as a single batch. Triplicate biological replicates were 
performed for each treatment condition. Drug efficacy (IC 50) was 
assessed bimonthly on at least two cell lines to ensure reproducibility. 
Single-drug experiments used a standard 10 μM concentration based 
on precedents from large-scale screening studies25,26,50. Combination 
treatment concentrations were informed by a comprehensive assay of 
2,025 drug combinations24. Following treatment, cells were washed 3 
times with PBS and lysed in buffer containing 6 M urea, 2 M thiourea and 
10 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Proteins were reduced and alkylated 
using tris-2(-carboxyethyl)-phosphine and iodoacetamide, respec-
tively, then digested with trypsin.
FFPE clinical sample collection and proteomics preparation
FFPE breast tumour specimens were collected from 501 patients 
who underwent surgery between January 2012 and December 2019 
(TNBC-HMU). All specimens were diagnosed as TNBC on the basis of 
immunohistochemical assessment of oestrogen receptor (ER), pro-
gesterone receptor (PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 
2 (HER2) status, and graded according to WHO classification guide-
lines by two experienced pathologists. Patients were followed until 
August 2025. For each patient, one 5-μm tumour slice with a greater 
than 70% tumour area was sectioned from FFPE blocks for proteomic  
analysis.
Protein extraction and peptide digestion were performed as 
described in ref. 51. In brief, FFPE tissues were dewaxed with heptane 
and rehydrated using gradient ethanol. Proteins were extracted and 
enzymatically digested into peptides using trypsin and endoproteinase 
lysine-C (Hualishi Scientific) with pressure cycling technology (Pres-
sure Biosciences Inc.).
Liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry 
analysis
For the proteomics analysis, 1 μg of purified peptides was injected 
into an Eksigent Nano LC 415 system (equipped with a 1–10 μl min−1 
flow module for nano-to-micro-flow switching). Chromatographic 
separation used an Eksigent analytical column (C18 ChromXP, 3 μm, 
0.3 × 150 mm) and trap column (C18 ChromXP, 5 μm, 0.3 × 10 mm) as 
described in ref. 52. Mobile phases consisted of buffer A (2% acetonitrile 
with 0.1% formic acid) and buffer B (98% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic 
acid). A linear gradient of 5–32% buffer B was applied over 15 min at 
5 μl min−1. DIA-MS analysis was performed on a TripleTOF 5600+ system 
with ion accumulation times of 150 ms for MS1 (m/z 350–1,250) and 
20 ms per DIA window. The optimized DIA scheme included 71 variable 
windows in high sensitivity mode.
For spectral library generation, 10 mg of peptides from various TNBC 
cell lines were fractionated using the Ultimate 3000 system following 
established protocols53. The resultant TNBC-specific spectral library 
encompassed 130,130 peptides corresponding to 9,355 proteins.
In DIA-MS analysis, a pooled sample of nine TNBC cell lines (BT20, 
BT549, HCC1143, HCC1395, HCC1806, HCC1937, HCC38, HCC70 and 
Hs578T) and randomly selected technical replicates served as quality 
control samples in each batch.
Proteomics data processing and quality control
Raw DIA data files were converted to profile mode mzML format using 
msConvert and analysed with DIA-NN software 54 (version 1.8.1) as  
previously described in refs. 55,56. Analysis parameters included the 
following: MS2 and MS1 mass accuracies and scan window size set to 
auto; trypsin/P as the protease with a maximum of two missed cleavages 
allowed; N-terminal methionine excision, cysteine carbamidomethyl-
ation and methionine oxidation as the specified modifications; peptide 
length restricted to a range of 7 to 30 amino acids; and m/z ranges of 
400 to 2,000 for precursor ions and 100 to 2,000 for fragment ions. 
Precursors and peptides were filtered at q values less than 0.01, retain-
ing only proteotypic peptides. Protein abundances were quantified 
using the MaxLFQ algorithm (DIA-NN R package) with q < 0.01 at the 
protein level. Data were processed in the single-pass mode using the 
neural network classifier.
Samples with less than 1,000 protein identifications (n = 689) were 
excluded, retaining 16,311 samples for analysis. No proteins had more 
than 90% missing values; the overall missing rate was 51.7%. Reproduc-
ibility of biological replicates, technical replicates and pooled samples 
was assessed using Pearson correlation and coefficient of variation.
PertScore calculation and empirical FDR estimation
Group comparisons were performed using a two-sided unpaired 
Welch’s t-test, with P values corrected for multiple testing using the 
Benjamini–Hochberg (B–H) method.
T o quantify the overall perturbation effect on each protein across 
several experimental dimensions, we defined a composite perturba-
tion score (PertScore). For each differential comparison defined by the 
drug, cell line and time point combinations, proteins were assigned 
a score of +1 or −1 if they showed significant differential abundance 
with consistent directionality, and 0 otherwise. The scores from all 
comparisons were summed to obtain the observed perturbation score 
for each protein (Sobserved).

mFuzz analysis
One-way ANOVA was used to determine differences between samples 
treated at different time points (P < 0.05). For the average normalized 
protein quantities, z-scores were used for fuzzy c-means clustering 
with the R (version 4.2.3) package mFuzz (version 2.58.0). The number 
of clusters was set to three, and the fuzzifier coefficient, M, was set to 
1.25. The clusters with consistently increasing or decreasing trends 
were selected (Supplementary Table 3b). In Fig.  2b, the clustering 
was based on the drug group, whereas in Fig. 2c, the clustering was 
based on both the drug group and drug sensitivity. The proteins that 
overlapped between the increasing clusters in the sensitive group and 
the decreasing clusters in the resistant group were filtered out. This 
filtration process is depicted in the upper panel of Fig. 2c, and the same 
procedure was conducted in reverse for the other group.
Unless otherwise stated, n denotes the number of biological repli-

group, n = 712 and N-resistant group, n = 607.
Data preprocessing for ProteinTalks pretraining
After obtaining data from different cell lines, various drug perturba-
tions and many time points, we averaged the samples from the repeated 
experiments: for example, data for 6 h in HCC70 with drug 75. Before 
inputting the data into the model, we performed min–max normali-
zation on each sample. Computational preprocessing used NumPy 
(version 1.24.2) and pandas (version 2.3.1). Ultimately, this process 
yielded the expression levels of 5,585 proteins under each perturbation 
condition, across different cell lines, at 0 h, 6 h, 24 h and 48 h.

ProteinTalks pretraining on PTDS
The dataset was split into training, validation and test sets with propor-
tions of 70%, 20% and 10%, respectively, using a sample-level random 
split and without stratification by cell line or experimental condition. 
The model was trained jointly to reconstruct time-resolved proteomes 
and predict single-drug efficacy or combination synergy. The model 
was trained with a batch size of 64. Early stopping used a patience of 
500 validation epochs before stochastic weight averaging-Gaussian 
(SWAG), increasing to 1,000 validation epochs during the SWAG phase. 
Training used the AdamW optimizer. The initial learning rate was set to 
0.0005, and the learning rate was adjusted using a ReduceLROnPlateau 
scheduler (mode = min, factor = 0.1, patience = 100) on the basis of 
validation loss. SWAG was implemented using torch.optim.swa_utils 
to improve model robustness and uncertainty estimation. The SWAG 
phase began after the validation loss plateaued and the learning rate 
dropped below 20% of its initial value; it used a fixed learning rate of 
0.0005, and snapshots were collected every epoch. A maximum of 20 
models were stored for covariance estimation. T o ensure reproducibil-
ity, a random seed of 1,995 was set for all random number generators, 
including those in PyT orch and NumPy.
Time-course perturbation proteomics and model training
We generated mtPTDS by profiling protein expression at 11 time points 
(0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 10 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h and 60 h) across 3 TNBC 
cell lines (HCC1806, HCC1143 and MDA-MB-453-1) treated with 55 single 
agents and 57 drug combinations. In total, mtPTDS comprised 2,528 
proteomic samples, including 41 pooled samples, 84 technical repli-
cates and 49 biological replicates used for quality control and repro-
ducibility assessment. After accounting for these quality control and 
replicate samples, 2,354 independent proteomic samples remained.
The mtPTDS dataset was randomly partitioned into training, valida-
tion and test subsets in a 0.7:0.2:0.1 ratio. T o model temporal responses, 
we extended the neural ODE component of the ProteinTalks framework 
to accommodate trajectories with up to ten post-baseline observations 
(that is, all time points excluding t = 0). During training, the model 
predicted the proteome at each scheduled time point; the predicted 
trajectory, together with the observed baseline proteome (t = 0), was 
then jointly used as input for downstream drug-efficacy prediction.

By concatenating data from many time points and combining it with 
drug data, we modified the input data dimensions of the DeepSyn -

that integrates omics data and drug information into the model to 
predict the efficacy of drug combinations. Other parameters were 
kept as original. We then applied DeepSynergy to our data. Drug pairs

Hyperparameter search for ProteinTalks and benchmark models
We performed a discrete factorial sweep over key training and archi-
tectural hyperparameters (Supplementary Table 4) to ensure repro-
ducible comparisons with alternative methods. The search spanned 
hidden-layer width {32, 64, 128}, dropout {0, 0.1, 0.2}, optimizer {SGD, 
AdamW}, learning rate {1 × 10−4, 5 × 10−4} and batch size {64, 128}, with 
weight decay fixed at 1 × 10−4. In total, 35 training runs (32 unique config-
urations) were evaluated. Each configuration was assessed on a held-out 
validation set using AUROC, along with accuracy, F1 score, Matthews 
correlation coefficient and cross-entropy loss. The top-performing 
region consistently used AdamW with a learning rate of 5 × 10−4, hidden 
width 64 to 128, batch size 64 to 128 and low or no dropout (0–0.2). The 
best single setting by AUROC (0.927) used AdamW (5 × 10−4), hidden 
width 64, batch size 64, dropout 0 and weight decay 1 × 10−4.
For other methods, we tuned four baselines—bagged decision trees, 
random forests, KNN and DeepSynergy—across five predefined evalu-
ation splits. For each model, we performed a discrete grid over a few 
high-impact hyperparameters. The search spaces are as follows:
• Bagging (DecisionTreeRegressor base): nestimators ∈ {10,100}; base-tree 
max depth fixed at 5; bootstrap enabled.
• Random forest: nestimators ∈ {10,100}; max_depth ∈ {20,None}; max_ 
features ∈ {sqrt, log2}; min_samples_split = 2.
• KNN: k ∈ {3,5}; weights ∈ {distance, uniform}.
• DeepSynergy (multilayer perceptron): hidden width ∈ {4096,8192}; 
dropout ∈ [0.28, 0.42]; learning rate ∈ [7.4 × 10−3, 9.1 × 10−3].
Metrics calculation for model performance evaluation
For each predictive task, model performance was evaluated using accu-
racy, AUPRC and AUROC. Metrics were computed with scikit-learn 
(version 1.3.0) and torcheval (version 0.0.7). For each metric, the mean 
performance across independent training repeats was calculated, 
and the standard error (s.e.)/standard deviation (s.d.) was reported to 
quantify variability under different random seeds and data splits. All 
numerical results, including those in Supplementary Tables 5–7, 9 and 
12, are presented with corresponding standard deviations or standard 
errors to reflect performance variability across runs.
Leave-one-cell-line-out evaluation
T o assess cross-cell-line generalization, we performed leave-one-  
cell-line-out experiments across 18 cell lines, while restricting perfor-
mance evaluation to 17 evaluable cell lines. DU4475 was excluded only 
from the held-out evaluation folds because all its labels were negative, 
but it remained eligible for inclusion in the training or validation data 
when other cell lines were held out. In each fold, one evaluable cell 
line was withheld as the test set, and the remaining cell lines consti-
tuted the development pool. The development pool was randomly 
partitioned into training and validation subsets using a fixed random 
seed; the held-out cell line was never used for model fitting or tuning. 
Performance was computed on each held-out evaluable cell line and 
aggregated across 17 folds.
Leave-one-drug-out evaluation
T o quantify generalization across compound identities, we con -
ducted leave-one-drug-out using single-agent treatments with avail-
able ground-truth labels. Of the 63 profiled single-agent treatments, 
compound 3 lacked ground-truth labels and was excluded from model 
training, validation and testing. Thus, leave-one-drug-out analysis was 
performed over the remaining 62 single-agent treatments. In each fold, 
one drug was held out as the test set, with all remaining single-agent 
data forming the development pool. Within each fold, the development 
pool was randomly split into training and validation subsets using the 
same fixed random seed; no samples associated with the test drug 
were used during training or validation. The trained model was then 
evaluated on the held-out drug, and prediction accuracy was recorded. 
Performance was summarized by aggregating test-set accuracies across 
all 62 folds.
Independent testing on PTNC
T o construct PTNC, we performed cell viability assays to evaluate the 
drug sensitivity of four TNBC cell lines (BT549, HCC1806, HS578T and 
MDA-MB-468) to 81 anticancer compounds. For each drug–cell line pair, 
cells were treated at four concentration points, and three independent 
biological replicate experiments were performed. Drug sensitivity 
labels were derived from the resulting dose–response measurements. 
T o ensure label reliability, we excluded drug–cell line pairs with incon-
sistent results across replicates or ambiguous sensitivity classification. 
After filtering, PTNC contained 2,076 drug-dose–response measure-
ments, corresponding to 173 high-confidence drug–cell line combina-
tions, which were used as an independent perturbation proteomics 
dataset for downstream analyses.
We evaluated generalization to untrained drugs using PTNC. We  
then applied the frozen ProteinTalks model to PTNC without fine-  
tuning to predict efficacy for each drug–cell line pair. The same train-
ing and testing protocol was used for all baselines: train and vali -
dation on PTDS and evaluation on PTNC. Performance is shown in the  
main text.
Few-shot transfer on PTPC
PTPC was generated as an extra independent perturbation proteom-
ics dataset across five cancer cell lines (A375, HCT116, NCI-H1975, 
NCI-H2170 and Panc 10.05). Cells were treated with 708 repurposed 
compounds, and proteomic profiles were collected at 6 h and 24 h 
after treatment. The dataset comprised 2,307 proteomic samples in 
total, including 90 pooled samples, 86 technical replicates and 110 
biological replicates, which were used for quality control and assess-
ment of technical and biological reproducibility. After accounting 
for these quality control and replicate samples, PTPC contained 2,021 
independent proteomic samples for model evaluation.
For few-shot transfer evaluation, proteomic profiles from the 6-h 
and 24-h time points were combined for each perturbation before 
model training and evaluation, yielding 983 labelled perturbation-level 
samples across the 5 cell lines. For each cell line, samples were strati-
fied by the binary outcome and partitioned into training, validation 
and test subsets in a 4:1:5 ratio. The resulting per-cell-line splits were 
then pooled across all five cell lines to form the final training, valida-
tion and test sets used for model fitting, model selection and held-out 
evaluation, respectively. Class prevalences varied substantially across 
cell lines, with sample counts (positive fraction) as follows: A375, n = 94 
(0.21); HCT116, n = 600 (0.05); NCI-H1975, n = 94 (0.35); NCI-H2170, 
n = 100 (0.32) and Panc 10.05, n = 95 (0.23). Owing to this imbalance, 
the AUPRC was used as the primary performance metric.

Drug-efficacy SHAP value calculation for each protein
The contribution of individual proteins in relation to sensitivity to 
a single drug or the synergy of a drug combination was determined 
using SHAP values for Loss 2 (ref. 61). The analysis commenced by cal-
culating the SHAP value for each protein, thereby assessing its specific 
contribution to the predictive model’s output. For each cell line and 
drug condition, the SHAP values of each protein were computed for 
sensitive drugs or effective drug combinations, using the resistant 
drugs or non-synergistic combinations from other cell lines or drugs 
as background samples. This evaluation framework permitted the 
assessment of the roles of proteins in predicting the effectiveness of 
drugs or their combinations.
SHAP value filtration
For each trained predictor (single-drug sensitivity or combination 
synergy), we computed sample-level SHAP values for all protein fea-
tures and took the absolute value to quantify importance irrespec -
tive of effect direction. Protein-wise importance was then aggregated 
by averaging absolute SHAP values across all samples. Proteins were 
ranked by this mean absolute SHAP magnitude, and the top 100 were 
designated as the ‘top 100 SHAP set’ . This set constitutes a core feature 
panel enriched for proteins with the greatest inferred influence on 
drug efficacy (single agents) or synergy (combinations) and was used 
for downstream interpretation and analyses.
T o account for the uncertainty inherent in SHAP estimation, we 
quantified the variability of SHAP values across biological replicates 
corresponding to the same drug (or drug combination) but derived 
from different cell lines. Specifically, the s.d. of SHAP values was com-
puted to capture sampling variability arising from heterogeneous 
samples and the opposite-label background sets used across cell lines 
and drug conditions. This s.d. reflects how consistently the model 
attributes importance to each protein under repeated perturbations 
within the same pharmacological context, thereby providing a statis-
tically grounded measure of uncertainty for the SHAP-based feature 
ranking (Supplementary Table 11a).

Fine-tuning with a pan-cancer PDX cohort (PDX-2015nm)
We evaluated cross-domain generalization from cell lines to PDX using 
the PDX-2015nm pan-cancer dataset40. ProteinTalks was first trained 
on PTDS and then used to initialize a transfer step in which the model 
was adapted solely on the breast cancer subset of PDX-2015nm (cell 
line to PDX adaptation). No samples from non-breast tumour types in 
PDX-2015nm were used during adaptation, hyperparameter tuning 
or model selection. The adapted model was subsequently applied, 
without any further fine-tuning, to the remaining non-breast tumour 
types in PDX-2015nm to obtain zero-shot predictions.
For comparative assessment, all baseline models underwent the 
identical train-adapt-evaluate protocol (pretraining on PTDS, adap-

performance on the zero-shot targets was reported according to the 
evaluation metrics defined in the main text.

PDO culture and drug-efficacy assay
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medi -
cal University Cancer Hospital (KY2025-33). TNBC specimens were 
provided by the Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Fresh 
tumour samples were immediately placed in tissue storage solution 
after surgical resection and transported to the laboratory at 4 °C 
for immediate processing. Digested tumour tissues were filtered 
through a 70-μm filter and centrifuged at 300g for 3 min. The pellet 
was resuspended with Matrigel (356231, Corning) and dispensed into 
a 24-well cell culture dish. A volume of 50 μl of Matrigel-cell mixture 
was seeded per well on a pre-warmed 24-well plate. After 30 min of 
solidification of Matrigel, a volume of 50 μl of conditioned medium 
was then added. The culture medium was replenished every 3–4 days, 
and organoids were passaged every 1–2 weeks using TrypLE Express  
(Gibco).
Subsequently, TNBC organoids were harvested and digested into 
single cells and counted with 0.4% Trypan Blue solution. Organoids 
were resuspended in Matrigel and embedded in 96-well plates (roughly 
5,000 cells per well). After the Matrigel was solidified, culture medium 
was added and organoids were grown to 70–100 μm. The concentra-
tions used were 0 μM, 0.0001 μM, 0.005 μM, 0.05 μM, 0.5 μM, 5 μM, 
50 μM, 100 μM and 200 μM, triplicate wells were used for each drug 
concentration. The complete medium was then replaced with culture 
medium with different concentrations of chemotherapy drugs, includ-
ing JNK-IN-7, THZ-1, cisplatin, CAY10603 and citarinostat. Cell viabil-
ity was assessed using CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay 
(G9683, Promega). After 2 days of drug incubation, the luminescence 
of each well was measured.
Drug library generation for PDO drug-efficacy prediction
Starting from the ProteinTalks-derived seed set of potentially effec-
tive small molecules for three given PDOs, identified using PTDS, we 
performed structure-based expansion within a reference library of 
3,000 compounds. The reference library was standardized to canoni-
cal SMILES, deduplicated by canonical SMILES and filtered to remove 
exact seed-set matches. Seed and reference molecules were encoded 
as ECFP6 (radius 3; 2,048 bits; RDKit), and pairwise structural similarity 
was computed using the Tanimoto coefficient. For each seed com-
pound, the five most similar reference molecules were selected; the 
union of these neighbours constituted the expanded candidate library. 
Candidates were then rescored using the same prediction model and 
scoring pipeline as the initial screen, and high-confidence hits were 
retained by a unified threshold or rank-based cut-off.
Drug-efficacy prediction for a TNBC PDO
The expanded candidate library was crossed with the PDO panel to form 
a compound-PDO matrix. Efficacy was predicted in silico using two 
classes of ProteinTalks models: (1) two models pretrained on cell-line 
proteomics and (2) the same architectures further adapted on PDX 
data. All models were applied without extra tuning to generate per-pair 
efficacy scores. For each PDO, outputs were combined by means of a 
score-averaging ensemble to yield a consensus ranking of putatively 
effective compounds. Candidates lacking literature-supported clinical 
evidence were then prioritized for prospective validation in organoid 
assays.
